Welcome, Shape Detective! 🕵️

Your mission: investigate the claim “If two rectangles have the same perimeter, they must have the same area.”

Learning goals
  1. Distinguish perimeter (boundary length) from area (covered surface).
  2. Use examples and counterexamples to test a mathematical claim.
  3. Explain why equal perimeters do not guarantee equal areas.
  4. Use mathematical reasoning to maximise the area of a garden.

How to play

  1. Enter your name above.
  2. Complete the six missions in order (about 20–30 minutes).
  3. Read each instruction, then interact with the shapes or answer questions.
  4. Use Hint when stuck; read the feedback before moving on.
  5. Visit Detective Report to see your progress and print your reflection.

The six learning missions can work offline, but Google Forms require an internet connection. Mission progress is stored in this browser only. This is a learning prototype, not a validated assessment instrument.

Mission 2 • Conceptual foundations

📐 Build the Basics

Instructions: Study the grid. Perimeter measures the boundary in centimetres; area measures the surface in square centimetres. Answer both questions.

Rectangle: 5 cm × 3 cm

Perimeter = 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 cm

Area = 5 × 3 = 15 cm²

Notice the units: cm is a length, but cm² is a measure of surface.

Q1. Which unit is appropriate for area?

🔬 Advanced mathematical proof (teacher / curious detective)

For a fixed perimeter of 40 m, l + w = 20. Thus A = l(20 − l) = 100 − (l − 10)². Since every square is non-negative, A ≤ 100. Equality occurs at l = w = 10. Therefore the maximum area is 100 m².

This proof extends beyond the primary-school investigation and is useful for the project report's mathematical reasoning section.
